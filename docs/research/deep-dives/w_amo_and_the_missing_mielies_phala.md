## Summary (paraphrase)

Every day after school Amo counts the six mielies in her grandmother's garden. One day there are five, then four; Koko guesses the birds Amo loves are eating them. Instead of chasing the birds away, Koko covers the cobs with cut plastic bottles, so the mielies are safe and the birds can still be watched.

## Repetition

- refrain: Amo's call to the mielies before each count — functions participation, rhythm; effective: True — it opens the ritual so the listener knows a count is coming
- predictable_pattern: Counting the cobs aloud: to six, then breaking off at five, then at four — functions participation, anticipation; effective: True — the pattern sets an expectation and its breaking is the plot; a child can count along and feel the gap
- phrase: The call to lunch returns, once as a welcome and once as Koko's postponement — functions memory; effective: None — the second use carries mild tension, but it is easy to miss

## Visual storytelling

- The culprit is in the pictures from the first spread: birds over the cobs, a bird's tail and spilled kernels on page 6, wings around the counting hand on page 7, feathers on page 9.
- Plastic bottles stand on Koko's kitchen shelf on page 5, long before they become the solution on page 11.
- The solution's mechanism (each cob inside a cut bottle) is shown only in the last picture.
- Counting pages print the numerals large so a child can count along with the drawn cobs and fingers.

## Strengths

- a counting pattern whose breaking is the plot
- picture clues let a child solve the mystery before the adult names it
- the solution keeps both loved things (mielies and birds) instead of chasing a 'villain'
- very short sentences and a clear daily ritual

## Weaknesses

- the adult finds and executes the solution; Amo only notices and asks
- Koko postpones the problem once without reason

## Anti-patterns observed

- adult_rescues_child (pages [11, 12]): Koko has the idea and the result; Amo's contribution is noticing and asking a humane question.
