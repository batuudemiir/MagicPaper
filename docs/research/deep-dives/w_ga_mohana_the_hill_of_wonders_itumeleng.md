## Summary (paraphrase)

A boy reading with his grandmother asks whether giraffes or lions live on the hill outside; she says no and takes him to see what does, and on the walk they meet a dassie, a hare and birds before he finds a golden stone she calls a tiger's eye.

## Repetition

- structure: two rounds of a storybook-animal question answered with a gentle no — functions anticipation, rhythm; effective: True — the second round is predictable, so the break into 'let's go and see' lands as a turn
- phrase: Luvo's call to his grandmother to look returns on the dassie and stone pages — functions participation, rhythm; effective: True — marks his two own discoveries; the second one is the peak
- callback: the title phrase returns as the last line — functions memory, comfort; effective: True — closes the book on its own name

## Visual storytelling

- The animals Luvo imagines (giraffe, lion) are drawn as his own soft toys sitting on a rock; the real animals are drawn in a different, naturalistic way.
- The window frame of page 1 returns on page 5 with the two hands reaching across it: inside turns to outside.
- Pages 8-9 are quiet: whispered and soft speech, few figures, open white space before the stone peak.
- The page 11 picture puns on the stone's name with a tiger's eye in leaves.

## Strengths

- imagined animals are answered by real ones: the child's own place becomes the wonder
- a gentle question-and-no cycle that breaks into action
- the peak is the child's own find, named by the grandmother

## Weaknesses

- no problem to solve: stakes are curiosity only
- the grandmother initiates the walk; the child's agency is in noticing
